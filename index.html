Unit 1.1

What econometrics is, and why study it

Definition
Econometrics uses economic theory and data from economics, business and the social sciences, together with tools from statistics, to answer "how much" questions. Put briefly, it is the use of statistical methods to analyse economic data.

Econometrics fills the gap between being a student of economics and being a practising economist. It lets you say to an employer:

  • "I can predict the sales of your product."
  • "I can estimate the effect on your sales if your competitor lowers its price by RM1 per unit."
  • "I can test whether your new advertising campaign is actually increasing sales."

"How much" questions decision-makers face

  • A city council: how much will violent crime fall if an extra million is spent on uniformed police?
  • A university: how much will enrolment fall if tuition rises by a given amount, and will tuition revenue rise or fall?
  • A transport authority: how will a fare increase affect the number of travellers who switch to car or bike, and what happens to revenue?
  • An investor: how much of your savings should go into a stock fund versus the money market, given forecasts of activity, inflation and interest rates?

Typical goals of econometric analysis

Estimate

Estimate relationships and parameters between economic variables, such as an elasticity.

Test

Test economic theories and hypotheses, such as whether newspaper advertising beats store displays.

Predict / evaluate

Forecast outcomes (GDP, inflation, interest rates, college enrolment) and evaluate government and business policy.

Key point: econometricians typically analyse non-experimental data. That single fact explains much of what makes econometrics different from statistics in the laboratory sciences.

Unit 1.2

Economic models and econometric models

Econometric analysis usually moves through two steps: (1) an economic model, which is sometimes skipped, and (2) an econometric model.

An economic model may be micro or macro. It often rests on optimising behaviour or equilibrium, and it establishes relationships between economic variables, for example demand equations or pricing equations. We express these ideas with a function.

Example: Becker's (1968) economic model of crime

Derived from utility maximisation, the model says hours spent in criminal activity depend on:

y = f(x1, x2, x3, x4, x5, x6, x7)

y = hours in criminal activity; x1 = "wage" from crime; x2 = wage in legal employment; x3 = other income; x4 = probability of being caught; x5 = probability of conviction if caught; x6 = expected sentence; x7 = age.

The functional form is not specified. The equation could also have been written down without formal economic modelling.

From economic to econometric model

To turn this into something we can estimate we must (a) choose a functional form, (b) replace unobservable variables with measurable proxies, and (c) add an error term. Click each term below to see what it means.

Systematic part and random part

Every econometric model has two components:

  • A systematic part, which comes from economic theory and includes an assumption about functional form.
  • A random, unpredictable component (u in Wooldridge, e in Hill et al.). It is "noise" that obscures our view of the relationship.

The coefficients β are unknown parameters that we estimate from data. The functional form is itself a hypothesis, and one challenge is to choose a form that is compatible with both theory and the data. Most of econometrics deals with the specification of the error u: what is in it, and how it relates to the explanatory variables.

Using the model for statistical inference

  • Estimating economic parameters, such as elasticities.
  • Predicting economic outcomes, such as enrolment in two-year colleges over the next ten years.
  • Testing hypotheses. In the wage equation, β3 is the effect of training on wages: how large is it, and is it different from zero?

Unit 1.3

How are data generated?

We must have data. Economists work in a complex world where data on variables are observed and rarely obtained from a controlled experiment. That makes learning about economic parameters harder.

Experimental data

The researcher controls (ideally randomly assigns) the level of the variable of interest and observes outcomes. Rare in business and the social sciences, but they exist. A notable example is Tennessee's Project STAR, where students were randomly assigned to small or regular-sized classes.

Non-experimental data

Values are neither fixed nor repeatable. In survey data, for example, data on all variables are collected simultaneously. Most economic data, such as labour force surveys, national accounts and financial prices, are of this kind.

Why it matters: with experimental data, random assignment makes the treatment unrelated to other factors, so a simple comparison has a causal meaning. With non-experimental data, the variable of interest may be correlated with factors in u, and naïve comparisons can mislead. Unit 1.5 develops this idea.

Unit 1.4

Structures and types of economic data

Econometric methods depend on the nature of the data. Using inappropriate methods can give misleading results. There are four main data structures. Select one to see an example from the lecture tables.

Other ways to describe data

Aggregation

Micro: individuals, households, firms. Macro: aggregated across a whole economy or region.

Stock vs flow

Flow: measured over a period of time, such as GDP for 2025. Stock: measured at a point in time, such as the money supply on 31 December.

Quantitative vs qualitative

Quantitative: expressed as numbers. Qualitative: an "either-or" situation, usually coded as a 0/1 dummy (such as female, married).

Unit 1.5

Causality and the notion of ceteris paribus

Causal effect of x on y
How does variable y change if variable x is changed, but all other relevant factors are held constant?

Ceteris paribus means "other relevant factors being equal." Most economic questions are ceteris paribus questions. It is important to define which causal effect you are interested in, and it is useful to describe how an experiment would have to be designed to infer that causal effect.

Four classic cases

Experiment feasible

Fertilizer and crop yield

Randomly assign fertilizer amounts to one-acre plots and compare yields. It works because the amount applied is unrelated to land quality, rainfall or parasites.

Experiment infeasible

Return to education

We cannot randomly assign years of schooling. Without random assignment, education is related to other factors that affect wages, such as ability, family background and experience.

Experiment infeasible

Police and city crime

No two cities are alike except in police numbers. More importantly, police numbers are determined at the same time as the crime rate (simultaneity).

Experiment infeasible

Minimum wage and unemployment

Randomly setting the minimum wage would work in theory. In reality its level depends on political and economic factors that also affect unemployment.

Thinking tool: for any research question, ask "What is the ideal randomised experiment?" Then ask "Why can't I run it, and what does that imply about the factors hiding in u?" The answer tells you what can go wrong with a simple regression.

Unit 1.6

The research process, writing, and data sources

Econometrics is ultimately a research tool. You will either do research, read and evaluate other people's, or both. Research flows in an orderly pattern:

  1. Use economic theory to think about the problem.
  2. Develop a working economic model leading to an econometric model.
  3. Obtain sample data and choose a method of statistical analysis based on initial assumptions and how the data were collected.
  4. Estimate the unknown parameters with statistical software, make predictions and test hypotheses.
  5. Perform model diagnostics to check the validity of assumptions.
  6. Analyse and evaluate the economic consequences and implications of the results.

A research proposal abstract should include

  • A concise statement of the problem
  • Available information, with one or two key references
  • The research design: economic model; econometric estimation and inference methods; data sources; estimation, testing and prediction procedures, including software version
  • The potential contribution of the research

Typical outline of a research report

  1. Statement of the problem
  2. Review of the literature
  3. The economic model
  4. The econometric model
  5. The data
  6. Estimation and inference procedures
  7. Empirical results and conclusions
  8. Extensions and limitations
  9. Acknowledgments
  10. References

Writing forces you to distil ideas, and nothing reveals your depth of understanding as clearly. A style guide such as The Chicago Manual of Style or Turabian's A Manual for Writers of Research Papers, Theses, and Dissertations is a good investment.

Sources of economic data

Finding a source is not the same as obtaining the data, and obtaining data is often easier than understanding what it means.

Gateways

Databases
